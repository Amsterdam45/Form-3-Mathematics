Lesson 1 of 7

Simplifying Algebraic Fractions

Builds on: Unit 5: Factorization (common factors, difference of two squares, trinomials). Simplifying numerical fractions.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can simplify an algebraic fraction by factorising the top and the bottom and cancelling common factors, and I know which cancellations are NOT allowed.

1 Key ideas

  • An algebraic fraction has algebra in the top (numerator), the bottom (denominator), or both. We write (x + 1)/(x − 2) for ‘x + 1 divided by x − 2’. Put brackets round the top and bottom when they have more than one term.
  • It works exactly like a numerical fraction: 12/18 = (2 × 6)/(3 × 6) = 2/3. We divide the top and bottom by the same common factor. This is called cancelling.
  • Step 1: Factorise the numerator and the denominator completely. Use a common factor, the difference of two squares, or a trinomial from Unit 5. Step 2: Cancel every factor that appears on both the top and the bottom. Step 3: write what is left.
  • You can only cancel factors (things multiplied together). You can never cancel terms (things added or subtracted). So (x + 4)/4 does NOT simplify to x + 1, because x + 4 cannot be written as a product with 4 as a factor.
  • Opposite factors: (x − 3) and (3 − x) are opposites, because 3 − x = −(x − 3). So (x − 3)/(3 − x) = −1.
  • A denominator can never be zero. The simplified fraction is equal to the original only for values of x that keep the original denominator from being zero. For (x² − 9)/(x − 3), we need x ≠ 3.
  • Check by substitution. Pick a number for x (not one that makes a denominator zero). The original fraction and your simplified answer must give the same value.

3 Worked examples

Example 1.1

Cancelling numbers and letters

Simplify (a) 12x/(18x²) (b) (6x + 12)/9

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) 12x/(18x²): divide top and bottom by 6 to get 2x/(3x²). Then divide by x to get 2/(3x).
  2. (b) Factorise the top: 6x + 12 = 6(x + 2). The fraction is 6(x + 2)/9.
  3. Divide the top and bottom by 3: 2(x + 2)/3.

Answer: (a) 2/(3x) (b) 2(x + 2)/3

Think: Why can you not cancel the 2 in 2(x + 2)/3 with the 3?

Example 1.2

Taking out a common factor

Simplify (x² + 5x)/x².

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Factorise the top: x² + 5x = x(x + 5).
  2. The fraction is x(x + 5)/x². Write x² as x × x.
  3. Cancel one x from the top and the bottom.

Answer: (x + 5)/x

Think: Check with x = 2. Does (4 + 10)/4 equal (2 + 5)/2?

Example 1.3

Difference of two squares

Simplify (x² − 9)/(x + 3).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Factorise the top: x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3).
  2. The fraction is (x − 3)(x + 3)/(x + 3).
  3. Cancel the factor (x + 3).

Answer: x − 3 (for x ≠ −3)

Example 1.4

Trinomials on top and bottom

Simplify (x² + 5x + 6)/(x² − 4).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Top: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).
  2. Bottom: x² − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2).
  3. The fraction is (x + 2)(x + 3)/((x − 2)(x + 2)). Cancel (x + 2).

Answer: (x + 3)/(x − 2)

Example 1.5

A common factor first

Simplify (2x² − 8)/(x² + x − 6).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Top: take out 2 first: 2x² − 8 = 2(x² − 4) = 2(x − 2)(x + 2).
  2. Bottom: x² + x − 6 = (x + 3)(x − 2).
  3. Cancel (x − 2).

Answer: 2(x + 2)/(x + 3)

Example 1.6

Opposite factors

Simplify (a) (x − 5)/(5 − x) (b) (2x − 6)/(3 − x)

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) 5 − x = −(x − 5). So (x − 5)/(5 − x) = (x − 5)/(−(x − 5)) = −1.
  2. (b) Top: 2x − 6 = 2(x − 3). Bottom: 3 − x = −(x − 3). So the fraction is 2(x − 3)/(−(x − 3)) = −2.

Answer: (a) −1 (b) −2

Example 1.7

What you cannot do

A student says (x + 4)/4 = x + 1. Show that this is wrong.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Test with x = 4. Left side: (4 + 4)/4 = 2.
  2. Right side: 4 + 1 = 5.
  3. 2 ≠ 5, so the statement is false. The 4 in the top is added to x, so it is a term, not a factor. The correct simplification is not possible, but x/4 + 1 is a correct way to split the fraction.

Answer: False: the 4 on top is a term, not a factor

Common mistakes

  • Cancelling terms instead of factors: (x + 4)/4 = x + 1 is wrong. Cancel only things that are multiplied.
  • Cancelling before factorising, so that a common factor stays hidden in the top or bottom.
  • Forgetting the opposite-factor rule: (x − 3)/(3 − x) = −1, not 1.
  • Cancelling x² with x as ‘x² ÷ x = 1’. It is x² ÷ x = x.

Lesson 2 of 7

Adding, Subtracting, Multiplying and Dividing Algebraic Fractions

Builds on: Lesson 1, and the rules for adding and multiplying numerical fractions.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can write a sum or difference of algebraic fractions as one fraction, and multiply and divide algebraic fractions, simplifying the answer.

1 Key ideas

  • Adding and subtracting: first write every fraction over a common denominator, then add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator. Use the lowest common denominator (LCD): the smallest expression that every denominator divides into.
  • With numbers in the denominator, find the LCM: x/2 + x/3 = 3x/6 + 2x/6 = 5x/6.
  • With letters in the denominator, the LCD includes every different factor: the LCD of 1/x and 1/(x + 1) is x(x + 1). For 3/x and 5/(2x) it is 2x.
  • When you subtract, put brackets round the whole top of the fraction being subtracted. The minus sign changes every term inside: (x + 1)/2 − (x − 1)/3 = (3(x + 1) − 2(x − 1))/6.
  • Multiplying: multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms. It is easier to factorise and cancel first, before you multiply out.
  • Dividing: keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, and flip the second fraction. a/b ÷ c/d = a/b × d/c.
  • Always simplify your final answer (Lesson 1), and leave it as a single fraction with the denominator factorised.

3 Worked examples

Example 2.1

Numbers in the denominators

Write x/2 + x/3 as a single fraction.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The LCM of 2 and 3 is 6.
  2. x/2 = 3x/6 and x/3 = 2x/6.
  3. Add the tops: 3x + 2x = 5x.

Answer: 5x/6

Example 2.2

Subtracting with brackets

Write (x + 1)/2 − (x − 1)/3 as a single fraction.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The LCD is 6. Multiply the first top by 3 and the second top by 2.
  2. (3(x + 1) − 2(x − 1))/6.
  3. Expand carefully: 3x + 3 − 2x + 2 = x + 5.

Answer: (x + 5)/6

Think: Check with x = 1: the left side is 1 − 0 = 1. Does (1 + 5)/6 = 1?

Example 2.3

A letter in the denominator

Write 3/x + 5/(2x) as a single fraction.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The LCD is 2x.
  2. 3/x = 6/(2x).
  3. Add: 6/(2x) + 5/(2x) = 11/(2x).

Answer: 11/(2x)

Example 2.4

Two different brackets

Write 2/(x + 1) + 3/(x − 2) as a single fraction.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The LCD is (x + 1)(x − 2).
  2. 2/(x + 1) = 2(x − 2)/((x + 1)(x − 2)) and 3/(x − 2) = 3(x + 1)/((x + 1)(x − 2)).
  3. Add the tops: 2(x − 2) + 3(x + 1) = 2x − 4 + 3x + 3 = 5x − 1.

Answer: (5x − 1)/((x + 1)(x − 2))

Example 2.5

Subtracting, with a difference of two squares

Write 1/(x − 1) − 1/(x + 1) as a single fraction.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The LCD is (x − 1)(x + 1).
  2. The top is (x + 1) − (x − 1) = x + 1 − x + 1 = 2.
  3. (x − 1)(x + 1) = x² − 1.

Answer: 2/(x² − 1)

Example 2.6

Multiplying

Simplify (2x/(x − 3)) × ((x² − 9)/(4x)).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Factorise: x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3).
  2. The product is 2x(x − 3)(x + 3)/((x − 3) × 4x).
  3. Cancel 2x/4x to 1/2 and cancel (x − 3).

Answer: (x + 3)/2

Example 2.7

Dividing

Simplify (x/(x + 1)) ÷ (x²/(x² − 1)).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Change ÷ to × and flip: x/(x + 1) × (x² − 1)/x².
  2. Factorise: x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1).
  3. Cancel x and (x + 1): (x − 1)/x.

Answer: (x − 1)/x

Common mistakes

  • Adding the numerators and the denominators: 1/x + 1/y is NOT 2/(x + y).
  • Forgetting brackets when subtracting, so only the first term of the top changes sign.
  • Adding fractions with different denominators without a common denominator first.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing. Only the second fraction is flipped.

Lesson 3 of 7

Fractional Equations with Numbers in the Denominator

Builds on: Lesson 2, and solving equations from Unit 2 (brackets, unknowns on both sides).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve an equation that has a fraction on each side, and an equation with several fractions on one or both sides, by clearing the denominators.

1 Key ideas

  • Solve a fractional equation by getting rid of the fractions first. Multiply every term on both sides by the LCM of all the denominators.
  • One fraction on each side: you can cross-multiply: if a/b = c/d, then a × d = b × c. (This is the same as multiplying both sides by b × d.)
  • Several fractions: find the LCM of all the denominators (for 2, 3 and 4 it is 12). Multiply every term by it. Each fraction then becomes a whole expression.
  • Use brackets when you clear a fraction with more than one term on top: (x + 1)/2 × 6 = 3(x + 1). Expand the brackets carefully before you collect terms.
  • Remember to multiply the terms that have no fraction too: in (x + 1)/2 + (x − 1)/3 = 5, the 5 becomes 5 × 6 = 30.
  • After clearing, solve the equation as in Unit 2. Then check by substituting your answer into the original equation.
  • In a word problem, define x, write the equation, solve it, and answer in words.

Lesson 4 of 7

Fractional Inequalities with Numbers in the Denominator

Builds on: Lesson 3 (clearing fractions) and Unit 3: Inequalities (reversing the sign, number lines).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve an inequality with fractions on one or both sides, show the answer on a number line, and list integer solutions.

1 Key ideas

  • Solve a fractional inequality in the same way as a fractional equation: multiply every term by the LCM of the denominators to clear the fractions.
  • When the denominators are positive numbers, multiplying by the LCM does not change the inequality sign. For example, x/2 + 1 > 3 becomes x + 2 > 6.
  • Reverse the sign only when you multiply or divide by a negative number. This happens when x ends up with a negative coefficient, such as −x > 3, which gives x < −3.
  • Use brackets when you clear a fraction with more than one term on top, and be careful with a minus sign in front of a fraction.
  • A compound inequality can be solved by working on all three parts at once: −1 < (x + 1)/2 ≤ 3 becomes −2 < x + 1 ≤ 6, then −3 < x ≤ 5.
  • Show the answer on a number line: an open circle for < or >, a closed circle for ≤ or ≥. List the integer solutions carefully: they depend on whether the ends are included.
  • Check with a number from your answer (it must make the original true) and a number outside it (it must make it false).

Lesson 5 of 7

Fractional Equations with x in the Denominator

Builds on: Lessons 2 and 3, and solving linear equations.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve an equation with x in the denominator, state the values x cannot take, and reject any answer that makes a denominator zero.

1 Key ideas

  • A denominator can never be zero. Before you solve, find the restrictions: the values of x that make a denominator zero. For 5/(x − 3), x ≠ 3. For 1/(2x − 6), x ≠ 3. For 1/x, x ≠ 0.
  • Multiply every term on both sides by the LCD, which now contains x. For 1/x + 1/(2x) the LCD is 2x. For 2/x and 3/(x + 1) it is x(x + 1).
  • One fraction on each side: cross-multiply. 2/x = 3/(x + 1) becomes 2(x + 1) = 3x.
  • After clearing the fractions, you usually have a linear equation. Solve it as in Unit 2.
  • Always check your answer against the restrictions. If your answer is a value that makes a denominator zero, it is an extraneous solution and must be rejected. Sometimes this leaves no solution at all.
  • Why? Multiplying both sides by an expression that is zero for some value of x can create a false ‘solution’. The check removes it.
  • Substitute your final answer into the original equation to be sure both sides are equal.

  • If x² appears as the only thing in the denominator, such as 3/x² = 12, then x² = 1/4 and x = ±1/2. Remember both the positive and negative roots.
  • Some equations are true for every allowed x (an identity), and some have no solution. Be ready to see both.

  4. Check: 3/(1/4) = 12 ✓ for both.

Answer: x = 1/2 or x = −1/2

Example 6.2

x² and x together

Solve 1/x² = 4/x.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Restriction: x ≠ 0. The LCD is x².
  2. Multiply every term by x²: 1 = 4x.
  3. x = 1/4. Allowed. Check: 1/(1/16) = 16 and 4/(1/4) = 16 ✓

Answer: x = 1/4

Example 6.3

An equation that becomes a quadratic

Solve 3/x² + 2/x = 1.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Restriction: x ≠ 0. The LCD is x².
  2. Multiply every term by x²: 3 + 2x = x².
  3. Rearrange: x² − 2x − 3 = 0, so (x − 3)(x + 1) = 0.
  4. x = 3 or x = −1. Both are allowed. Check x = 3: 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 ✓. Check x = −1: 3 − 2 = 1 ✓

Answer: x = 3 or x = −1

Example 6.4

Factorising a difference of two squares

Solve 1/(x − 1) + 1/(x + 1) = 4/(x² − 1).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1). Restrictions: x ≠ 1 and x ≠ −1. The LCD is (x − 1)(x + 1).
  2. Multiply every term: (x + 1) + (x − 1) = 4.
  3. 2x = 4, so x = 2. Allowed. Check: 1 + 1/3 = 4/3 and 4/3 ✓

Answer: x = 2

Example 6.5

An answer to reject

Solve x/(x + 2) − 3/(x − 2) = 8/(x² − 4).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x² − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2). Restrictions: x ≠ 2 and x ≠ −2.
  2. Multiply every term by (x − 2)(x + 2): x(x − 2) − 3(x + 2) = 8.
  3. x² − 2x − 3x − 6 = 8, so x² − 5x − 14 = 0, so (x − 7)(x + 2) = 0.
  4. x = 7 or x = −2. But x = −2 is not allowed, so reject it. Check x = 7: 7/9 − 3/5 = 8/45 and 8/45 ✓

Answer: x = 7 (x = −2 rejected)

Example 6.6

Factorising a denominator first

Solve 2/(x² − 1) + 1/(x − 1) = 3/(x + 1).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1). Restrictions: x ≠ ±1.
  2. Multiply every term by (x − 1)(x + 1): 2 + (x + 1) = 3(x − 1).
  3. x + 3 = 3x − 3, so 6 = 2x, so x = 3. Allowed. Check: 2/8 + 1/2 = 3/4 and 3/4 ✓

Answer: x = 3

Example 6.7

The x² terms cancel

Solve (2x + 1)/(x − 1) = (2x + 3)/(x + 2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Restrictions: x ≠ 1 and x ≠ −2. Cross-multiply: (2x + 1)(x + 2) = (2x + 3)(x − 1).
  2. Expand: 2x² + 5x + 2 = 2x² + x − 3.
  3. The 2x² cancels: 4x = −5, so x = −5/4.

Answer: x = −5/4

Think: Why is there no quadratic to solve, even though x² appeared?

Common mistakes

  • Giving only x = +1/2 for x² = 1/4. There are two square roots.
  • Not factorising the denominators first, so the LCD becomes far too large.
  • Forgetting to check both answers of a quadratic against the restrictions.
  • Multiplying by x² but only multiplying some of the terms.

Lesson 7 of 7

Fractional Inequalities with x in the Denominator

Builds on: Lessons 4 and 5, and Unit 3: Inequalities (number lines, compound and ‘or’ inequalities).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve an inequality with x in the denominator by finding critical values and using a sign table, and I can show the answer on a number line.

1 Key ideas

  • Do not multiply both sides by an expression with x in it, unless you know it is positive. If it is negative, the sign must reverse, and you do not know which case you are in. For example, 1/x < 1 does NOT mean x > 1.
  • Method: (1) Move everything to one side so the other side is 0. (2) Write it as one fraction. (3) Find the critical values: where the top is zero, and where the bottom is zero. (4) Make a sign table to find the sign of the fraction in each interval. (5) Choose the intervals that match the inequality.
  • A fraction is positive if the top and bottom have the same sign, and negative if they have different signs. It is zero only when the top is zero.
  • Critical values from the bottom are never part of the answer (the fraction is undefined there), so they always get an open circle. Critical values from the top are included only if the sign is ≤ or ≥.
  • Squares are safe: x² and (x − 2)² are positive for every allowed x, so you may multiply by them without reversing the sign. For 4/x² > 1, multiply by x² (x ≠ 0) to get 4 > x².
  • The answer can be two separate pieces on the number line. Write it with ‘or’, for example x < 0 or x > 1.
  • Always test a number in each piece of your answer, and one number outside, in the original inequality.

3 Worked examples

Example 7.1

Moving to one side

Solve 6/x > 2.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Subtract 2: 6/x − 2 > 0. As one fraction: (6 − 2x)/x > 0.
  2. Critical values: the top is zero at x = 3, the bottom is zero at x = 0 (open circle).
  3. Sign table (see the figure): the fraction is positive only between 0 and 3.
  4. Check x = 1: 6 > 2 ✓. Check x = −1: −6 > 2 is false ✓. Check x = 4: 1.5 > 2 is false ✓

Answer: 0 < x < 3

Example 7.2

A fraction compared with zero

Solve (x + 1)/(x − 2) ≥ 0.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Critical values: x = −1 (top is zero) and x = 2 (bottom is zero).
  2. The fraction is positive when the top and bottom have the same sign: for x < −1 and for x > 2.
  3. It is zero at x = −1, which is allowed because of ≥. x = 2 is never allowed.
  4. So x ≤ −1 (closed circle) or x > 2 (open circle).

Answer: x ≤ −1 or x > 2

Example 7.3

A constant over a bracket

Solve 3/(x − 1) < 2.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. 3/(x − 1) − 2 < 0. As one fraction: (3 − 2(x − 1))/(x − 1) = (5 − 2x)/(x − 1) < 0.
  2. Critical values: x = 5/2 and x = 1 (open).
  3. From the sign table, the fraction is negative for x < 1 and for x > 5/2.
  4. Check x = 0: −3 < 2 ✓. Check x = 2: 3 < 2 is false ✓. Check x = 3: 1.5 < 2 ✓

Answer: x < 1 or x > 5/2

Example 7.4

A square in the denominator

Solve 4/x² > 1.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x² is positive for every x except 0, so we can multiply by x² without reversing the sign.
  2. 4 > x², so x² < 4, so −2 < x < 2.
  3. Remove x = 0, because 4/0 is undefined.

Answer: −2 < x < 2, x ≠ 0

Think: Check x = 1 and x = 3. Which satisfies 4/x² > 1?

Example 7.5

Two fractions

Solve 2/(x + 1) ≥ 1/(x − 1).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Move to one side: 2/(x + 1) − 1/(x − 1) ≥ 0.
  2. One fraction: (2(x − 1) − (x + 1))/((x + 1)(x − 1)) = (x − 3)/((x + 1)(x − 1)) ≥ 0.
  3. Critical values: x = −1 and x = 1 (open, from the bottom) and x = 3 (closed, from the top).
  4. The sign table gives positive for −1 < x < 1 and for x > 3, and zero at x = 3.

Answer: −1 < x < 1 or x ≥ 3

Example 7.6

A common trap

Solve 1/x < 1.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Do not multiply by x. Write 1/x − 1 < 0, which is (1 − x)/x < 0.
  2. Critical values: x = 1 (top) and x = 0 (bottom, open).
  3. The sign table shows negative for x < 0 and for x > 1.
  4. Check x = −1: −1 < 1 ✓. Check x = 1/2: 2 < 1 is false ✓. Check x = 2: 1/2 < 1 ✓

Answer: x < 0 or x > 1

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying both sides by x (or by (x − 2)) and not reversing the sign when it is negative.
  • Including a critical value from the denominator in the answer. It is always excluded.
  • Writing ‘x > 0 and x < 0’ or one long interval when the answer is two separate pieces.
  • Forgetting to move everything to one side so that the other side is exactly 0.
