Lesson 1 of 6

What Simultaneous Equations Mean

Builds on: Unit 2: substitution and solving equations. Unit 1: the intersection of two sets.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can explain what the solution of a pair of simultaneous equations is, check a solution, and find one from a graph.

1 Key ideas

  • An equation with two unknowns, such as x + y = 5, has many solutions: (1, 4), (2, 3), (3, 2), (0.5, 4.5) and so on.
  • Simultaneous equations are two equations that must be true at the same time. We look for the pair of values (x, y) that works in both.
  • Think of Unit 1: the solution is the intersection of the two solution sets.
  • To check a solution, substitute it into both equations.
  • On a graph, each equation is a straight line. The solution is the point where the two lines intersect.
  • Graphs can be inaccurate when the answer is not a whole number. That is why we also learn algebraic methods (Lessons 2–4).

3 Worked examples

Example 1.1

Finding a common solution

Find whole-number solutions of x + y = 5 and of x − y = 1. Which pair works for both?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x + y = 5: (0, 5), (1, 4), (2, 3), (3, 2), (4, 1), (5, 0).
  2. x − y = 1: (1, 0), (2, 1), (3, 2), (4, 3), (5, 4).
  3. The only pair in both lists is (3, 2).

Answer: x = 3, y = 2

Think: The lists are like two sets, and the answer is their intersection. Why can there be only one pair in both lists here?

Example 1.2

Checking a solution

Is x = 4, y = −1 the solution of 2x + y = 7 and x − 3y = 7?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. First equation: 2(4) + (−1) = 8 − 1 = 7 ✓.
  2. Second equation: 4 − 3(−1) = 4 + 3 = 7 ✓.
  3. It works in both equations, so it is the solution.

Answer: Yes

Example 1.3

Reading a graph

The graph shows the lines x + y = 5 and x − y = 1. Use it to solve the equations.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The lines cross at the point (3, 2).
  2. So x = 3 and y = 2.
  3. Check: 3 + 2 = 5 ✓ and 3 − 2 = 1 ✓.

Answer: x = 3, y = 2

Example 1.4

From words to a pair of equations

Two numbers add up to 20 and differ by 6. Write two equations and find the numbers.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Let the numbers be x and y, with x the larger one.
  2. x + y = 20 and x − y = 6.
  3. Try pairs that add to 20: 13 + 7 = 20 and 13 − 7 = 6 ✓.

Answer: x + y = 20, x − y = 6; the numbers are 13 and 7

Think: Guessing worked here. In Lesson 2 you will find a method that always works, even when the answers are not whole numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Checking a solution in only one equation.
  • Writing the coordinates the wrong way round. (3, 2) means x = 3 and y = 2.
  • Reading a graph carelessly. Always check your answer by substituting.

Lesson 2 of 6

Elimination by Adding or Subtracting

Builds on: Lesson 1, and collecting like terms (Unit 2).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve simultaneous equations by adding or subtracting them to eliminate one unknown.

1 Key ideas

  • Label the equations (1) and (2).
  • If one unknown has the same coefficient in both equations, you can eliminate it. Then there is only one unknown left, and you can solve for it as in Unit 2.
  • Same signs → subtract (for example, +2y and +2y). Different signs → add (for example, +y and −y). Remember it as "SSS: same signs subtract".
  • Substitute the value you found into one of the original equations to find the other unknown.
  • Check in the other equation. Then write the answer clearly: x = …, y = ….

3 Worked examples

Example 2.1

Adding to eliminate

Solve x + y = 10 (1) and x − y = 4 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The y terms are +y and −y (different signs), so add: (1) + (2): 2x = 14.
  2. x = 7.
  3. Substitute into (1): 7 + y = 10, so y = 3.
  4. Check in (2): 7 − 3 = 4 ✓

Answer: x = 7, y = 3

Example 2.2

Subtracting to eliminate

Solve 3x + 2y = 16 (1) and x + 2y = 8 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Both equations have +2y (same signs), so subtract: (1) − (2): 2x = 8.
  2. x = 4.
  3. Substitute into (2): 4 + 2y = 8, so 2y = 4 and y = 2.
  4. Check in (1): 12 + 4 = 16 ✓

Answer: x = 4, y = 2

Example 2.3

Adding with larger coefficients

Solve 5x − 3y = 11 (1) and 2x + 3y = 17 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. −3y and +3y have different signs, so add: 7x = 28, so x = 4.
  2. Substitute into (2): 8 + 3y = 17, so 3y = 9 and y = 3.
  3. Check in (1): 20 − 9 = 11 ✓

Answer: x = 4, y = 3

Example 2.4

Eliminating the first unknown

Solve 4a + b = 14 (1) and 4a − 3b = 6 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Both equations have +4a, so subtract: (1) − (2).
  2. b − (−3b) = 4b and 14 − 6 = 8, so 4b = 8 and b = 2.
  3. Substitute into (1): 4a + 2 = 14, so a = 3.
  4. Check in (2): 12 − 6 = 6 ✓

Answer: a = 3, b = 2

Think: When you subtract, b − (−3b) = 4b. Why is it 4b and not −2b?

Example 2.5

Negative answers

Solve 2x − y = 7 (1) and 3x − y = 10 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Both have −y (same sign), so subtract: (2) − (1): x = 3.
  2. Substitute into (1): 6 − y = 7, so y = −1.
  3. Check in (2): 9 − (−1) = 10 ✓

Answer: x = 3, y = −1

Common mistakes

  • Adding when you should subtract (or the other way round). Look at the signs.
  • Sign errors when subtracting negatives, e.g. 3y − (−y) = 4y.
  • Finding only one unknown. You need both.

Lesson 3 of 6

Elimination by Multiplying First

Builds on: Lesson 2: eliminating by adding or subtracting.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can multiply one or both equations so that one unknown can be eliminated.

1 Key ideas

  • If no unknown has matching coefficients, multiply one or both equations to make them match.
  • Multiply every term in the equation, including the number on the right-hand side.
  • Choose the unknown that is easier to match. Use the lowest common multiple of its two coefficients.
  • Then add or subtract as in Lesson 2 (same signs subtract, different signs add).
  • If everything cancels and you get something like 0 = 0, the two equations describe the same line, so there are infinitely many solutions. If you get something impossible, like 0 = 2, the lines are parallel and there is no solution.

3 Worked examples

Example 3.1

Multiplying one equation

Solve x + 2y = 7 (1) and 3x + y = 11 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Match the y terms: multiply (2) by 2 to get 6x + 2y = 22 (3).
  2. (3) − (1): 5x = 15, so x = 3.
  3. Substitute into (1): 3 + 2y = 7, so y = 2.
  4. Check in (2): 9 + 2 = 11 ✓

Answer: x = 3, y = 2

Example 3.2

Multiplying both equations

Solve 3x + 2y = 12 (1) and 2x + 3y = 13 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. To match the y terms, the LCM of 2 and 3 is 6.
  2. (1) × 3: 9x + 6y = 36 (3). (2) × 2: 4x + 6y = 26 (4).
  3. (3) − (4): 5x = 10, so x = 2.
  4. Substitute into (1): 6 + 2y = 12, so y = 3.
  5. Check in (2): 4 + 9 = 13 ✓

Answer: x = 2, y = 3

Example 3.3

Different signs

Solve 4x − 3y = 1 (1) and 5x + 2y = 30 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Match the y terms (LCM of 3 and 2 is 6): (1) × 2: 8x − 6y = 2. (2) × 3: 15x + 6y = 90.
  2. The signs are different, so add: 23x = 92, so x = 4.
  3. Substitute into (1): 16 − 3y = 1, so 3y = 15 and y = 5.
  4. Check in (2): 20 + 10 = 30 ✓

Answer: x = 4, y = 5

Example 3.4

Choosing which unknown to eliminate

Solve 5x + 3y = 21 (1) and 2x − y = 4 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Eliminating y is easier: multiply (2) by 3 to get 6x − 3y = 12.
  2. Add this to (1): 11x = 33, so x = 3.
  3. Substitute into (2): 6 − y = 4, so y = 2.
  4. Check in (1): 15 + 6 = 21 ✓

Answer: x = 3, y = 2

Think: Could you eliminate x instead? What would you multiply by? Which way is quicker?

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying only the left-hand side: (x + 2y = 7) × 3 is 3x + 6y = 21, not 3x + 6y = 7.
  • Choosing multipliers that don't make the coefficients match.
  • Forgetting to check the sign before deciding whether to add or subtract.

Lesson 4 of 6

The Substitution Method

Builds on: Lessons 2–3. Unit 4: changing the subject. Unit 2: expanding brackets.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve simultaneous equations by substitution, and choose the better method.

1 Key ideas

  • If one equation already gives one unknown in terms of the other (for example, y = 2x + 1), substitute that expression into the other equation.
  • This leaves one equation with one unknown. Solve it, then substitute back to find the other unknown.
  • Put the substituted expression in brackets: 2x − (x + 3), not 2x − x + 3.
  • If neither equation is in that form, you can first make one letter the subject (Unit 4). This is easiest when a letter has coefficient 1.
  • Choosing a method. Use substitution when a letter is already the subject, or when it has coefficient 1. Use elimination when both equations are in the form ax + by = c.
  • Substitution also works when one equation is not linear. For example, y = x² and y = x + 2 give x² = x + 2, which you can solve by factorizing (Unit 5).

3 Worked examples

Example 4.1

Substituting an expression

Solve y = 2x + 1 (1) and 3x + y = 16 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Replace y in (2) with 2x + 1: 3x + (2x + 1) = 16.
  2. 5x + 1 = 16, so x = 3.
  3. Substitute into (1): y = 2(3) + 1 = 7.
  4. Check in (2): 9 + 7 = 16 ✓

Answer: x = 3, y = 7

Example 4.2

Substituting for x

Solve x = 3y − 2 (1) and 2x + 5y = 18 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Substitute into (2): 2(3y − 2) + 5y = 18.
  2. 6y − 4 + 5y = 18, so 11y = 22 and y = 2.
  3. x = 3(2) − 2 = 4.
  4. Check in (2): 8 + 10 = 18 ✓

Answer: x = 4, y = 2

Example 4.3

Rearrange first

Solve x + 2y = 9 (1) and 3x − y = 6 (2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. In (1), x has coefficient 1, so make x the subject: x = 9 − 2y.
  2. Substitute into (2): 3(9 − 2y) − y = 6.
  3. 27 − 6y − y = 6, so 7y = 21 and y = 3.
  4. x = 9 − 6 = 3.

Answer: x = 3, y = 3

Example 4.4

Both equations give y

Solve y = x + 3 and y = 2x − 1.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Both expressions are equal to y, so they are equal to each other: x + 3 = 2x − 1.
  2. 4 = x, so x = 4.
  3. y = 4 + 3 = 7.

Answer: x = 4, y = 7

Think: On a graph, this is where the lines y = x + 3 and y = 2x − 1 cross. What are the coordinates of that point?

Example 4.5

Same problem, two methods

Solve 2x + y = 8 and x − y = 1 by elimination AND by substitution.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Elimination: add the equations: 3x = 9, so x = 3 and y = 2.
  2. Substitution: from the second equation, x = y + 1. Then 2(y + 1) + y = 8, so 3y = 6, y = 2 and x = 3.
  3. Both methods give the same answer.

Answer: x = 3, y = 2

Common mistakes

  • Leaving out the brackets: 2x − x + 3 instead of 2x − (x + 3).
  • Substituting back into the same expression, which only gives you back what you already had.
  • Stopping after finding one unknown.

Lesson 5 of 6

Cramer's Rule (Using Determinants)

Builds on: Lessons 2–4, and substituting into formulae (Unit 2).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can solve a pair of simultaneous equations using Cramer's rule, and use the determinant D to tell when there is no single solution.

1 Key ideas

  • Cramer's rule is a formula for solving two simultaneous equations. It uses numbers called determinants. It is a shortcut for what elimination does every time.
  • Step 1: write both equations in standard form, with the x term, the y term, then = a number: ax + by = e and cx + dy = f. Rearrange first if you need to.
  • Determinant of a 2 × 2 grid: for the grid with a, b on the top row and c, d on the bottom row, the determinant is ad − bc. Multiply the diagonal from top-left to bottom-right, then subtract the product of the other diagonal.
  • Three determinants: D = ad − bc (the coefficients). Dx = ed − bf (replace the x-column a, c with e, f). Dy = af − ec (replace the y-column b, d with e, f).
  • The solution: x = Dx ÷ D and y = Dy ÷ D, as long as D is not 0.
  • If D = 0 there is no single solution. The lines are either parallel (no solution) or the same line (infinitely many solutions). Look at Dx and Dy: if both are 0, it is the same line; otherwise the lines are parallel.
  • Watch the order of subtraction and keep the signs of negative numbers. Always check your answer in both original equations.
  • Cramer's rule is fast when the numbers are small and the equations are in standard form. Elimination or substitution can be quicker when a letter already has coefficient 1 or is the subject.

3 Worked examples

Example 5.1

Setting up D, Dx and Dy

For 2x + y = 7 and 3x − 2y = 7, find D, Dx and Dy, then solve.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Match with ax + by = e and cx + dy = f: a = 2, b = 1, e = 7 and c = 3, d = −2, f = 7.
  2. D = ad − bc = (2)(−2) − (1)(3) = −4 − 3 = −7.
  3. Dx = ed − bf = (7)(−2) − (1)(7) = −14 − 7 = −21.
  4. Dy = af − ec = (2)(7) − (7)(3) = 14 − 21 = −7.
  5. x = Dx ÷ D = −21 ÷ −7 = 3. y = Dy ÷ D = −7 ÷ −7 = 1.
  6. Check: 2(3) + 1 = 7 ✓ and 3(3) − 2(1) = 7 ✓

Answer: x = 3, y = 1

Example 5.2

A straightforward case

Solve x + 2y = 8 and 3x − y = 3 using Cramer's rule.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. a = 1, b = 2, e = 8 and c = 3, d = −1, f = 3.
  2. D = (1)(−1) − (2)(3) = −1 − 6 = −7.
  3. Dx = (8)(−1) − (2)(3) = −8 − 6 = −14.
  4. Dy = (1)(3) − (8)(3) = 3 − 24 = −21.
  5. x = −14 ÷ −7 = 2 and y = −21 ÷ −7 = 3.
  6. Check: 2 + 6 = 8 ✓ and 6 − 3 = 3 ✓

Answer: x = 2, y = 3

Example 5.3

A negative answer

Solve 2x + 3y = −1 and x − y = 2.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. a = 2, b = 3, e = −1 and c = 1, d = −1, f = 2.
  2. D = (2)(−1) − (3)(1) = −2 − 3 = −5.
  3. Dx = (−1)(−1) − (3)(2) = 1 − 6 = −5.
  4. Dy = (2)(2) − (−1)(1) = 4 + 1 = 5.
  5. x = −5 ÷ −5 = 1 and y = 5 ÷ −5 = −1.
  6. Check: 2(1) + 3(−1) = −1 ✓ and 1 − (−1) = 2 ✓

Answer: x = 1, y = −1

Think: In Dy, the product (−1)(1) was subtracted. What would happen to the answer if you forgot the negative sign?

Example 5.4

Rearranging first

Solve y = 2x − 1 and 3x + 2y = 12.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Put the first equation in standard form: 2x − y = 1.
  2. a = 2, b = −1, e = 1 and c = 3, d = 2, f = 12.
  3. D = (2)(2) − (−1)(3) = 4 + 3 = 7.
  4. Dx = (1)(2) − (−1)(12) = 2 + 12 = 14.
  5. Dy = (2)(12) − (1)(3) = 24 − 3 = 21.
  6. x = 14 ÷ 7 = 2 and y = 21 ÷ 7 = 3.
  7. Check in the original: y = 2(2) − 1 = 3 ✓ and 3(2) + 2(3) = 12 ✓

Answer: x = 2, y = 3

Example 5.5

Answers that are not whole numbers

Solve 2x + y = 4 and x + 3y = 3.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. a = 2, b = 1, e = 4 and c = 1, d = 3, f = 3.
  2. D = (2)(3) − (1)(1) = 5.
  3. Dx = (4)(3) − (1)(3) = 9.
  4. Dy = (2)(3) − (4)(1) = 2.
  5. x = 9/5 = 1.8 and y = 2/5 = 0.4.
  6. Check: 2(1.8) + 0.4 = 4 ✓ and 1.8 + 3(0.4) = 3 ✓

Answer: x = 9/5 (1.8), y = 2/5 (0.4)

Think: Elimination would also work here. Which method do you think is quicker when the answers are fractions?

Example 5.6

When D = 0

Use Cramer's rule on (a) x + 2y = 4 and 2x + 4y = 3, and (b) x + 2y = 4 and 2x + 4y = 8.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) D = (1)(4) − (2)(2) = 0. So there is no single solution. Dx = (4)(4) − (2)(3) = 10, which is not 0, so the lines are parallel and there is no solution.
  2. (b) D = (1)(4) − (2)(2) = 0. Dx = (4)(4) − (2)(8) = 0 and Dy = (1)(8) − (4)(2) = 0. All three are 0, so the lines are the same line.
  3. Check: the second equation in (b) is the first one doubled, so any point on one line is on the other: infinitely many solutions.

Answer: (a) no solution (parallel lines) (b) infinitely many solutions (same line)

Example 5.7

Checking against elimination

Solve x + 2y = 8 and 3x − y = 3 by elimination, and compare with Cramer's rule.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Multiply the second equation by 2: 6x − 2y = 6.
  2. Add to the first equation: 7x = 14, so x = 2.
  3. Substitute: 2 + 2y = 8, so y = 3.
  4. This matches the Cramer's rule answer (2, 3).

Answer: x = 2, y = 3 (same answer)

Example 5.8

A word problem

At a fair, 2 adult tickets and 3 child tickets cost $55. 3 adult tickets and 1 child ticket cost $65. Find the cost of each ticket using Cramer's rule.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Let a be the adult price and c the child price: 2a + 3c = 55 and 3a + c = 65.
  2. D = (2)(1) − (3)(3) = −7.
  3. Da = (55)(1) − (3)(65) = 55 − 195 = −140.
  4. Dc = (2)(65) − (55)(3) = 130 − 165 = −35.
  5. a = −140 ÷ −7 = 20 and c = −35 ÷ −7 = 5.
  6. An adult ticket costs $20 and a child ticket costs $5.

Answer: Adult $20, child $5

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting in the wrong order. D is ad − bc, not bc − ad. Reversing it changes the sign of the answer.
  • Replacing the wrong column. For Dx, the x-column (a, c) is replaced by e, f. For Dy, the y-column (b, d) is replaced.
  • Not writing the equations in standard form first, so that a, b, c, d, e, f are misread.
  • Losing a negative sign when a coefficient is negative, such as b = −1.

Answer D = 0 means there is no single solution. In (a), D = 0, Dx = 0 and Dy = 0: the same line, so infinitely many solutions. In (b), D = 0 but Dx = (3)(4) − (2)(7) = −2 ≠ 0: parallel lines, so no solution.
11
Always, sometimes or never? ‘If D = 0, the simultaneous equations have no solution.’
Answer Sometimes. If D = 0 and the lines are parallel, there is no solution. If D = 0 and the lines are the same, there are infinitely many solutions.

Solving Problems with Simultaneous Equations

Builds on: Lessons 2–5, and forming equations (Unit 2, Lesson 5).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can form two equations from a word problem, solve them, and answer in context.

1 Key ideas

  • Step 1: choose letters for the two unknowns and say what they stand for, with units. For example, "Let p be the cost of a patty in dollars."
  • Step 2: write two equations, one from each fact in the question.
  • Step 3: solve using elimination or substitution.
  • Step 4: answer the question in words, with units.
  • Step 5: check your answer against the story, not just the equations. A negative number of people or a fraction of a ticket means something is wrong.
  • "More than" can be tricky. "An adult ticket costs $5 more than a child ticket" means a = c + 5. Test your equation with numbers.

3 Worked examples

Example 6.1

Sum and difference

Two numbers have a sum of 42 and a difference of 8. Find them.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Let the numbers be x and y, with x > y.
  2. x + y = 42 and x − y = 8.
  3. Add: 2x = 50, so x = 25 and y = 17.

Answer: 25 and 17

Example 6.2

Canteen prices

At the school canteen, 3 patties and 2 juices cost $24. 1 patty and 2 juices cost $12. Find the cost of a patty and of a juice.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Let a patty cost $p and a juice cost $j.
  2. 3p + 2j = 24 (1) and p + 2j = 12 (2).
  3. (1) − (2): 2p = 12, so p = 6.
  4. 6 + 2j = 12, so j = 3.
  5. Check: 3(6) + 2(3) = 24 ✓

Answer: A patty costs $6 and a juice costs $3.

Example 6.3

Concert tickets

200 tickets were sold for a concert in St John's. Adult tickets cost $25 and child tickets cost $10. The total taken was $3800. How many of each were sold?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Let a = number of adult tickets and c = number of child tickets.
  2. a + c = 200 and 25a + 10c = 3800.
  3. Substitute c = 200 − a: 25a + 10(200 − a) = 3800.
  4. 25a + 2000 − 10a = 3800, so 15a = 1800 and a = 120.
  5. c = 80. Check: 25(120) + 10(80) = 3000 + 800 = 3800 ✓

Answer: 120 adult tickets and 80 child tickets

Example 6.4

Ages

A father is 3 times as old as his son. In 10 years' time, he will be twice as old as his son. Find their ages now.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Let the son be s years old and the father f years old.
  2. Now: f = 3s. In 10 years: f + 10 = 2(s + 10).
  3. Substitute: 3s + 10 = 2s + 20, so s = 10.
  4. f = 30. Check: in 10 years they will be 20 and 40, and 40 = 2 × 20 ✓

Answer: The son is 10 and the father is 30.

Think: Why is "in 10 years" written as f + 10 and s + 10, and not just f + 10?

Common mistakes

  • Not saying what the letters stand for, which leads to mixed-up equations.
  • Writing "$5 more than" the wrong way round.
  • Giving x = 120, y = 80 without saying what the numbers mean.
