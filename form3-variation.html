Lesson 1 of 4

Direct Variation

Builds on: Unit 2: substitution and rearranging. Unit 3: making a letter the subject.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can write direct variation as y = kx, find k from a pair of values, and use it to find other values.

1 Key ideas

  • y varies directly as x (or y is directly proportional to x, written y ∝ x) means y = kx for some fixed number k, called the constant of variation.
  • As x gets bigger, y gets bigger in the same ratio — doubling x doubles y, trebling x trebles y.
  • To find k, substitute one known pair of values (x, y) into y = kx and solve for k. Once you know k, you can find any other value.
  • On a graph, direct variation is always a straight line through the origin (0, 0). If a graph does not pass through the origin, it is not direct variation, even if it is a straight line.
  • In a table, direct variation shows up as y ÷ x being the same number (equal to k) for every pair in the table.
  • Direct variation is a special kind of equation you already know from Unit 2 — it is just y = mx with c = 0.

3 Worked examples

Example 1.1

Finding k from a table

The table shows values of y that vary directly as x. Find k, then write the equation. x: 2, 4, 6, 8 y: 5, 10, 15, 20

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Check that y ÷ x is the same each time: 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5, 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5, 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5, 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5.
  2. So k = 2.5.
  3. The equation is y = 2.5x.

Answer: y = 2.5x

Example 1.2

Finding k from one pair of values

y varies directly as x. When x = 5, y = 30. Find the equation connecting y and x, and use it to find y when x = 9.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. y = kx, so 30 = k × 5.
  2. k = 6.
  3. The equation is y = 6x.
  4. When x = 9: y = 6 × 9 = 54.

Answer: y = 6x; y = 54 when x = 9

Example 1.3

Finding x when y is known

y varies directly as x. When x = 3, y = 21. Find x when y = 49.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. 30 = k × 5 is not this question — use the given pair: 21 = k × 3, so k = 7.
  2. y = 7x.
  3. 49 = 7x, so x = 7.

Answer: x = 7

Think: Notice this question asks you to find x, not y. The method is the same — just solve the equation for the letter you need.

Example 1.4

Recognizing direct variation from a graph

The graph shows the cost C of fuel in dollars for d litres. Which line shows direct variation, and why?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The teal line C = 2.5d passes through the origin: when d = 0, C = 0 (no fuel, no cost). This is direct variation.
  2. The coral line C = 15 + 2.5d does NOT pass through the origin — it starts at (0, 15). This is a fixed charge plus a rate, not direct variation, even though it is also a straight line.

Answer: The teal line (C = 2.5d) is direct variation, because it passes through the origin.

Think: What might the $15 represent in the coral line? (Hint: think of a delivery fee.)

Common mistakes

  • Thinking any straight line is direct variation. It must pass through the origin.
  • Forgetting to find k first, and guessing the equation instead.
  • Mixing up which value is x and which is y when substituting.

Lesson 2 of 4

Direct Variation with Powers

Builds on: Lesson 1. Unit 2: substitution with indices.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can write and use direct variation with a power, such as y = kx² or y = kx³.

1 Key ideas

  • Direct variation is not always y = kx. Sometimes y varies as a power of x, such as y ∝ x² ("y varies as the square of x") or y ∝ x³ ("y varies as the cube of x").
  • y ∝ x² means y = kx². As x doubles, y is multiplied by 2² = 4, not by 2.
  • y ∝ x³ means y = kx³. As x doubles, y is multiplied by 2³ = 8.
  • The method for finding k is the same as Lesson 1: substitute a known pair of values and solve for k — just remember to substitute x before squaring or cubing it, not after.
  • The graph of y = kx² (with k > 0) is a curve through the origin that gets steeper — not a straight line. It still starts at (0, 0), like all direct variation.
  • Watch the wording carefully: "y varies as the square of x" is y = kx², but "y varies as x, squared" would mean (y)² = kx — always translate from the words to the equation with care.

3 Worked examples

Example 2.1

Finding k for y ∝ x²

y varies directly as the square of x. When x = 2, y = 20. Find the equation, then find y when x = 4.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. y = kx², so 20 = k × 2² = k × 4.
  2. k = 5.
  3. The equation is y = 5x².
  4. When x = 4: y = 5 × 4² = 5 × 16 = 80.

Answer: y = 5x²; y = 80 when x = 4

Example 2.2

Finding x from y ∝ x²

y = 5x² (from the previous example). Find x when y = 45.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. 45 = 5x².
  2. x² = 9.
  3. x = 3 (taking the positive root, since x represents a length here).

Answer: x = 3

Think: Mathematically x² = 9 also gives x = −3. Why do we usually take only the positive answer in a real-world problem?

Example 2.3

y varies as the cube of x

The volume, V, of a cube varies directly as the cube of its side length, s. When s = 2 cm, V = 8 cm³. Find the equation, then find V when s = 5 cm.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. V = ks³, so 8 = k × 2³ = k × 8.
  2. k = 1.
  3. V = s³.
  4. When s = 5: V = 5³ = 125 cm³.

Answer: V = s³; V = 125 cm³

Think: Does this equation look familiar? It is the formula for the volume of a cube that you may already know.

Example 2.4

Comparing effects on y

y varies directly as x². If x is doubled, what happens to y? If x is trebled, what happens to y?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. y = kx². Replace x with 2x: k(2x)² = k × 4x² = 4(kx²) = 4y.
  2. So doubling x makes y 4 times as big.
  3. Replace x with 3x: k(3x)² = 9kx² = 9y.
  4. So trebling x makes y 9 times as big.

Answer: Doubling x makes y 4 times as big; trebling x makes y 9 times as big.

Think: This is why braking distance (which varies as speed squared) increases so quickly as a car speeds up.

Common mistakes

  • Squaring or cubing k instead of x. In y = kx², only x is squared.
  • Forgetting to square x before finding k: writing 20 = k × 2 instead of 20 = k × 2².
  • Assuming doubling x always doubles y — this is only true for y = kx, not y = kx² or y = kx³.

Lesson 3 of 4

Inverse Variation

Builds on: Lessons 1–2. Unit 3: rearranging a formula with a fraction.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can write inverse variation as y = k/x, find k, and use it to find other values.

1 Key ideas

  • y varies inversely as x (or y is inversely proportional to x) means y = k/x for some constant k. As x gets bigger, y gets smaller.
  • An equivalent way to write it is xy = k — the product of x and y stays the same for every pair of values.
  • To find k, substitute one known pair of values into y = k/x (or xy = k) and solve.
  • On a graph, inverse variation (with k > 0) is a curve that gets closer and closer to both axes but never touches them. It is not a straight line, and it never passes through the origin.
  • In a table, inverse variation shows up as x × y being the same number (equal to k) for every pair.
  • A classic example: if a job takes 12 worker-days, then more workers finish it in fewer days — the number of workers and the number of days vary inversely, with k = 12.

3 Worked examples

Example 3.1

Finding k from a table

The table shows values of y that vary inversely as x. Find k, then write the equation. x: 1, 2, 3, 4 y: 12, 6, 4, 3

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Check that x × y is the same each time: 1 × 12 = 12, 2 × 6 = 12, 3 × 4 = 12, 4 × 3 = 12.
  2. So k = 12.
  3. The equation is y = 12/x.

Answer: y = 12/x

Example 3.2

Finding k from one pair of values

y varies inversely as x. When x = 5, y = 8. Find the equation, then find y when x = 4.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. y = k/x, so 8 = k/5.
  2. k = 40.
  3. y = 40/x.
  4. When x = 4: y = 40/4 = 10.

Answer: y = 40/x; y = 10 when x = 4

Example 3.3

Finding x when y is known

y = 40/x (from the previous example). Find x when y = 8, and again when y = 20.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. When y = 8: 8 = 40/x, so 8x = 40 and x = 5 (this matches the pair we started with — a good check).
  2. When y = 20: 20 = 40/x, so 20x = 40 and x = 2.

Answer: x = 5 (checks the original pair); x = 2 when y = 20

Think: As y got bigger (from 8 to 20), x got smaller (from 5 to 2). Is that what you expect for inverse variation?

Example 3.4

Workers and days (a classic inverse variation)

It takes 6 workers 8 days to build a wall. The number of days, d, varies inversely as the number of workers, w. How many days would it take 4 workers, working at the same rate?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. d = k/w. Using w = 6, d = 8: 8 = k/6, so k = 48.
  2. d = 48/w.
  3. When w = 4: d = 48/4 = 12 days.

Answer: 12 days

Think: Fewer workers means the job takes longer — does 12 days (up from 8) make sense?

Common mistakes

  • Using y = kx (direct) by mistake instead of y = k/x (inverse) — check whether y increases or decreases as x increases.
  • Forgetting to multiply both sides by x when solving for k or for x.
  • Assuming an inverse variation graph is a straight line — it is a curve.

Lesson 4 of 4

Solving Problems with Variation

Builds on: Lessons 1–3, and forming equations from words (Unit 2, Lesson 5).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can decide whether a situation is direct or inverse variation, form the equation, find k, and solve the problem in context.

1 Key ideas

  • Step 1: Decide the type. Ask: as one quantity increases, does the other increase (direct) or decrease (inverse)? Or does the problem say "varies as the square/cube" (direct with a power)?
  • Step 2: Write the general equation — y = kx, y = kxⁿ, or y = k/x — using letters that match the quantities in the problem.
  • Step 3: Substitute the first pair of values given, and solve for k.
  • Step 4: Rewrite the equation with k replaced by its value.
  • Step 5: Substitute the new value given, and solve for the unknown.
  • Step 6: Answer in words, with the correct units, and check the answer makes sense: for direct variation both quantities should move the same way; for inverse variation they should move opposite ways.

3 Worked examples

Example 4.1

Deciding the type first

For each situation, say whether it is direct or inverse variation. (a) The cost of tickets and the number bought. (b) The time for a journey and the speed travelled, for a fixed distance.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) More tickets costs more money — both increase together, so this is direct variation.
  2. (b) A faster speed means less time — one increases while the other decreases, so this is inverse variation.

Answer: (a) direct (b) inverse

Example 4.2

A full direct-variation problem

The weight, W, of a wire varies directly as its length, L. A 3 m length weighs 450 g. Find the weight of a 7 m length.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Increasing L increases W, so this is direct variation: W = kL.
  2. 450 = k × 3, so k = 150.
  3. W = 150L.
  4. When L = 7: W = 1050 g.

Answer: 1050 g

Example 4.3

A full inverse-variation problem

The number of days, d, needed to dig a trench varies inversely as the number of workers, w. It takes 5 workers 12 days. How many days would 3 workers take?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Fewer workers means more days, so this is inverse variation: d = k/w.
  2. 12 = k/5, so k = 60.
  3. d = 60/w.
  4. When w = 3: d = 20 days.

Answer: 20 days

Think: Does it make sense that fewer workers (3, down from 5) need more days (20, up from 12)?

Example 4.4

Variation with a square, in context

The distance, d, a ball rolls down a ramp varies directly as the square of the time, t, since release. It rolls 2 m in 1 second. How far has it rolled after 3 seconds?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. "Varies as the square of the time" means d = kt².
  2. 2 = k × 1², so k = 2.
  3. d = 2t².
  4. When t = 3: d = 2 × 9 = 18 m.

Answer: 18 m

Example 4.5

Combining variation with algebra from earlier units

y varies inversely as x. When x = 6, y = 5. Find x when y = 2x (that is, when y equals twice x).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Find k: 5 = k/6, so k = 30. So y = 30/x.
  2. Set y = 2x: 2x = 30/x.
  3. Multiply both sides by x: 2x² = 30, so x² = 15 and x = √15 (taking the positive root).

Answer: x = √15 ≈ 3.87

Think: This question links variation to solving an equation, like the skills from Units 2 and 3. Which parts of your working came from variation, and which from equation-solving?

Common mistakes

  • Not deciding direct or inverse first, and guessing the wrong equation.
  • Forgetting units in the final answer.
  • Not checking that the answer moves in a sensible direction for the type of variation.

Investigate. For a fixed distance of 120 km, complete a table of speed (km/h) and time (hours) for speeds 20, 30, 40, 60. What kind of variation is this, and what is the constant?
Answer Inverse variation, k = 120 (the distance). Times: 6, 4, 3, 2 hours.
12
Challenge. y varies directly as x, and z varies inversely as y. When x = 4, y = 12. When y = 12, z = 3. Find z when x = 8.
Answer z = 1.5

1
y varies directly as x. When x = 6, y = 27.
  1. (a) Find the constant of variation, k, and write the equation connecting y and x. [2 marks]
  2. (b) Find y when x = 10. [1 mark]
Answer (a) k = 4.5, y = 4.5x (b) y = 45
2
y varies inversely as x. When x = 4, y = 9.
  1. (a) Find the constant of variation, k, and write the equation connecting y and x. [2 marks]
  2. (b) Find x when y = 6. [2 marks]
Answer (a) k = 36, y = 36/x (b) x = 6
3
The graph shows two relationships between x and y.
  1. (a) Which line shows direct variation? Give a reason. [1 mark]
  2. (b) Write the equation of the line that shows direct variation, and state the value of k. [2 marks]
Answer (a) The teal line, because it passes through the origin. (b) C = 2.5d, k = 2.5
4
The kinetic energy, E, of a moving object varies directly as the square of its speed, v. When v = 10 m/s, E = 500 J.
  1. (a) Find the equation connecting E and v. [2 marks]
  2. (b) Find E when v = 20 m/s. [2 marks]
  3. (c) Find v when E = 1125 J. [2 marks]
Answer (a) E = 5v² (b) E = 2000 J (c) v = 15 m/s

It takes 5 taps 36 minutes to fill a water tank, working at the same rate.
  1. (a) State whether the number of taps and the time taken vary directly or inversely, giving a reason. [1 mark]
  2. (b) Find the constant of variation and the equation connecting time, t, and the number of taps, n. [2 marks]
  3. (c) Find the time taken by 9 taps. [2 marks]
Answer (a) Inversely — more taps means less time. (b) k = 180, t = 180/n (c) t = 20 minutes
