Lesson 1 of 6

Taking Out a Common Factor

Builds on: Unit 2, Lesson 2: expanding brackets. Factorizing is the reverse.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can factorize an expression completely by taking out the highest common factor.

1 Key ideas

  • Factorizing means writing an expression as a product of factors. It is the reverse of expanding: expanding 6(x + 2) gives 6x + 12, so factorizing 6x + 12 gives 6(x + 2).
  • Find the highest common factor (HCF) of all the terms, including numbers and letters. Write it outside the bracket. Inside the bracket, write what each term becomes after dividing by the HCF.
  • Always take out a common factor first. It is the first step for every factorizing question in this unit.
  • "Factorize completely" (or "fully") means take out the highest common factor. 2x² + 4x = 2(x² + 2x) is not complete. 2x(x + 2) is.
  • Check by expanding your answer. You should get back to the original expression.
  • The common factor can be a whole bracket: a(x + 2) + 5(x + 2) = (x + 2)(a + 5). This idea is used in Lesson 2.

3 Worked examples

Example 1.1

A number as the common factor

Factorize 12x + 18.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The HCF of 12 and 18 is 6.
  2. 12x ÷ 6 = 2x and 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
  3. So 12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3).
  4. Check: 6 × 2x + 6 × 3 = 12x + 18 ✓

Answer: 6(2x + 3)

Example 1.2

A letter as the common factor

Factorize x² + 3x.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Both terms contain x, so x is a common factor.
  2. x² ÷ x = x and 3x ÷ x = 3.
  3. So x² + 3x = x(x + 3). The tile diagram shows a rectangle x by (x + 3).

Answer: x(x + 3)

Example 1.3

Factorizing completely

Factorize completely: (a) 2x² + 4x (b) 3x² + 6

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) The numbers have HCF 2 and both terms contain x, so the HCF is 2x.
  2. 2x² ÷ 2x = x and 4x ÷ 2x = 2, so 2x² + 4x = 2x(x + 2).
  3. (b) The HCF of 3 and 6 is 3, but 6 has no x, so x is not common. 3x² + 6 = 3(x² + 2).

Answer: (a) 2x(x + 2) (b) 3(x² + 2)

Think: 2(x² + 2x) is correct but not complete. How can you tell that a bracket still has a common factor inside it?

Example 1.4

More than one letter

Factorize completely 6a²b − 9ab².

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Numbers: HCF of 6 and 9 is 3.
  2. Letters: both terms have at least one a and at least one b, so ab is common.
  3. HCF = 3ab.
  4. 6a²b ÷ 3ab = 2a and 9ab² ÷ 3ab = 3b.
  5. Answer: 3ab(2a − 3b).

Answer: 3ab(2a − 3b)

Example 1.5

A bracket as the common factor

Factorize a(x + 2) + 5(x + 2).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The bracket (x + 2) appears in both terms, so it is a common factor.
  2. Take it out: (x + 2)(a + 5).

Answer: (x + 2)(a + 5)

Think: Expand (x + 2)(a + 5) to check. You will use this step in every question in Lesson 2.

Common mistakes

  • Not taking out the highest common factor: 6x² + 9x = 3(2x² + 3x) is not complete.
  • Forgetting the 1: 5x + 5 = 5(x + 1), not 5x.
  • Losing a sign inside the bracket. Check by expanding.

Lesson 2 of 6

Factorizing by Grouping

Builds on: Lesson 1: taking out a common factor, including a common bracket.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can factorize four-term expressions such as ax + bx + ay + by by grouping.

1 Key ideas

  • A four-term expression often has no factor common to all four terms, but pairs of terms do share a factor.
  • Method: (1) Group the terms in pairs. (2) Take out a common factor from each pair. (3) The two brackets should now match. Take out the common bracket.
  • ax + bx + ay + by = x(a + b) + y(a + b) = (a + b)(x + y).
  • If the brackets do not match, try rearranging the terms and grouping them differently.
  • When the second pair starts with a minus, take out a negative factor. For example, −3y + 6 = −3(y − 2). Check the signs by expanding.
  • Grouping also factorizes quadratics once the middle term has been split: x² + 3x + x + 3 = x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = (x + 1)(x + 3). You will use this in Lessons 5 and 6.

3 Worked examples

Example 2.1

The basic pattern

Factorize ax + ay + bx + by.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Group in pairs: (ax + ay) + (bx + by).
  2. Take a common factor from each pair: a(x + y) + b(x + y).
  3. The bracket (x + y) is common. Take it out: (x + y)(a + b).

Answer: (x + y)(a + b)

Example 2.2

A quadratic written with four terms

Factorize x² + 3x + 2x + 6.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Group: (x² + 3x) + (2x + 6).
  2. Take out factors: x(x + 3) + 2(x + 3).
  3. Take out the common bracket: (x + 3)(x + 2).

Answer: (x + 3)(x + 2)

Example 2.3

A number in front of x²

Factorize 3x² + 9x + 2x + 6.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Group: (3x² + 9x) + (2x + 6).
  2. Take out factors: 3x(x + 3) + 2(x + 3).
  3. Common bracket: (x + 3)(3x + 2).

Answer: (3x + 2)(x + 3)

Example 2.4

Taking out a negative factor

Factorize xy − 2x − 3y + 6.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Group: (xy − 2x) + (−3y + 6).
  2. First pair: x(y − 2).
  3. Second pair: take out −3 so that the bracket matches. −3y + 6 = −3(y − 2).
  4. Now x(y − 2) − 3(y − 2) = (y − 2)(x − 3).
  5. Check: expand (y − 2)(x − 3) = xy − 3y − 2x + 6 ✓

Answer: (y − 2)(x − 3)

Think: What would happen if you took out +3 from the second pair instead of −3?

Example 2.5

Rearranging first

Factorize ab + 6 + 2a + 3b.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Grouping as written gives (ab + 6) + (2a + 3b), which has no common factors.
  2. Rearrange so that each pair shares a letter: ab + 2a + 3b + 6.
  3. a(b + 2) + 3(b + 2) = (b + 2)(a + 3).

Answer: (b + 2)(a + 3)

Common mistakes

  • Stopping at x(a + b) + y(a + b). This is not factorized yet. You must take out the common bracket.
  • Sign errors when the third term is negative. Take out a negative factor and check.
  • Giving up when the brackets don't match. Try another order first.

Lesson 3 of 6

The Difference of Two Squares

Builds on: Lesson 1 and square numbers. Unit 2: expanding brackets.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can recognise and factorize a² − b².

1 Key ideas

  • a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b).
  • Why? Expand (a − b)(a + b) = a² + ab − ab − b² = a² − b². The middle terms cancel. The diagram shows the same fact with areas.
  • Look for: exactly two terms, both perfect squares, with a minus between them.
  • Write each term as something squared: 4x² − 9 = (2x)² − 3² = (2x − 3)(2x + 3).
  • Take out a common factor first: 2x² − 50 = 2(x² − 25) = 2(x − 5)(x + 5).
  • A sum of two squares, such as x² + 9, cannot be factorized with real numbers.
  • If one term is not a perfect square, you can still use surds: x² − 5 = (x − √5)(x + √5). You will not need this in Form 3.

3 Worked examples

Example 3.1

Why the pattern works

Factorize x² − 16 and check by expanding.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x² = (x)² and 16 = 4². It is a difference of two squares.
  2. So x² − 16 = (x − 4)(x + 4).
  3. Check: x² + 4x − 4x − 16 = x² − 16 ✓. The middle terms cancel.

Answer: (x − 4)(x + 4)

Example 3.2

A coefficient in front of x²

Factorize 4x² − 9.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. 4x² = (2x)² and 9 = 3².
  2. So 4x² − 9 = (2x − 3)(2x + 3).

Answer: (2x − 3)(2x + 3)

Example 3.3

Two letters

Factorize 25a² − 49b².

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. 25a² = (5a)² and 49b² = (7b)².
  2. So 25a² − 49b² = (5a − 7b)(5a + 7b).

Answer: (5a − 7b)(5a + 7b)

Example 3.4

Common factor first

Factorize completely 3y² − 75.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. 75 is not a perfect square, but 3 is a common factor: 3(y² − 25).
  2. y² − 25 = (y − 5)(y + 5).
  3. So 3y² − 75 = 3(y − 5)(y + 5).

Answer: 3(y − 5)(y + 5)

Example 3.5

Mental arithmetic

Work out 53² − 47² without a calculator.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Use a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b) with a = 53 and b = 47.
  2. (53 − 47)(53 + 47) = 6 × 100 = 600.

Answer: 600

Think: Why is this much quicker than working out 53² and 47² separately?

Common mistakes

  • Writing (x − 3)² for x² − 9. (x − 3)² = x² − 6x + 9.
  • Trying to factorize a sum of squares: x² + 25 ≠ (x + 5)(x + 5).
  • Forgetting to square-root the coefficient: 9x² − 4 = (3x − 2)(3x + 2), not (9x − 2)(9x + 2).

Lesson 4 of 6

Perfect Square Trinomials

Builds on: Lesson 3 and Unit 2, Lesson 4, where you found that (a + b)² ≠ a² + b².

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can recognise and factorize perfect squares such as x² + 8x + 16 and 9x² − 24x + 16.

1 Key ideas

  • (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b².
  • The tile diagram shows (x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9. There is one x² square, two strips of 3x, and a 3 × 3 square of ones.
  • How to spot one: (1) the first and last terms are perfect squares, (2) the last term is positive, and (3) the middle term = 2 × (√first) × (√last).
  • x² + 8x + 16: √x² = x, √16 = 4, and 2 × x × 4 = 8x ✓. So x² + 8x + 16 = (x + 4)².
  • If the middle term is negative, the bracket has a minus: x² − 10x + 25 = (x − 5)².
  • In Unit 8 (Completing the Square), you will learn to turn any quadratic into a perfect square plus or minus a number.

3 Worked examples

Example 4.1

Seeing a perfect square

Expand (x + 3)² using the tile diagram.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (x + 3)² = (x + 3)(x + 3).
  2. The diagram shows 1 x² tile, 6 x tiles (3 across the top and 3 down the side), and 9 unit tiles.
  3. So (x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9.

Answer: x² + 6x + 9

Think: Why does the middle term always come out as 2 × x × 3?

Example 4.2

Factorizing a perfect square

Factorize x² + 8x + 16.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. First term: x² = (x)². Last term: 16 = 4².
  2. Check the middle term: 2 × x × 4 = 8x ✓.
  3. So x² + 8x + 16 = (x + 4)².

Answer: (x + 4)²

Example 4.3

A negative middle term

Factorize x² − 10x + 25.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. x² = (x)² and 25 = 5².
  2. 2 × x × 5 = 10x, and the middle term is −10x, so use a minus sign.
  3. x² − 10x + 25 = (x − 5)².

Answer: (x − 5)²

Example 4.4

A coefficient in front of x²

Factorize (a) 4x² + 20x + 25 (b) 9x² − 24x + 16.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) 4x² = (2x)² and 25 = 5². 2 × 2x × 5 = 20x ✓. So (2x + 5)².
  2. (b) 9x² = (3x)² and 16 = 4². 2 × 3x × 4 = 24x, and it is negative. So (3x − 4)².

Answer: (a) (2x + 5)² (b) (3x − 4)²

Example 4.5

Is it a perfect square?

(a) Is x² + 6x + 4 a perfect square? (b) What number c makes x² + 12x + c a perfect square?

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) If it were, it would be (x + 3)², because 2 × 3 = 6. But (x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9, not + 4. So it is not a perfect square.
  2. (b) Half of 12 is 6, and 6² = 36. So c = 36, and x² + 12x + 36 = (x + 6)².

Example 4.6

Common factor first

Factorize completely 2x² + 12x + 18.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Take out 2: 2(x² + 6x + 9).
  2. x² + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)².
  3. So 2x² + 12x + 18 = 2(x + 3)².

Answer: 2(x + 3)²

  • Writing (x + 4)(x − 4) for x² + 8x + 16. That is a difference of squares, which has no middle term.

Lesson 5 of 6

Factorizing Quadratics x² + bx + c

Builds on: Lesson 2 (grouping) and Lesson 4 (perfect squares).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can factorize any quadratic x² + bx + c that has whole-number factors.

1 Key ideas

  • Expanding shows the pattern: (x + p)(x + q) = x² + (p + q)x + pq.
  • So to factorize x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. This is called factorizing by inspection.
  • Signs: if c is positive, both numbers have the same sign as b. If c is negative, the numbers have opposite signs, and the larger one takes the sign of b.
  • List the factor pairs of c in an organised way so that you don't miss any.
  • The tile diagram shows x² + 5x + 6 as a rectangle (x + 2) by (x + 3).
  • Another method: split bx into two terms using the numbers you found, then group (Lesson 2). x² + 5x + 6 = x² + 2x + 3x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).
  • Always check your answer by expanding.

Example 5.1

Two positive numbers

Factorize x² + 5x + 6.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5.
  2. Factor pairs of 6: 1 × 6 (sum 7) and 2 × 3 (sum 5 ✓).
  3. So x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).

Example 5.2

Listing factor pairs

Factorize x² + 13x + 36.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Factor pairs of 36: 1 × 36 (sum 37), 2 × 18 (sum 20), 3 × 12 (sum 15), 4 × 9 (sum 13 ✓), 6 × 6 (sum 12).
  2. So x² + 13x + 36 = (x + 4)(x + 9).

Answer: (x + 4)(x + 9)

Example 5.3

Both numbers negative

Factorize x² − 7x + 12.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. c = +12 is positive, so both numbers have the same sign. b = −7 is negative, so both are negative.
  2. −3 × −4 = 12 and −3 + (−4) = −7 ✓.
  3. So x² − 7x + 12 = (x − 3)(x − 4).

Answer: (x − 3)(x − 4)

Example 5.4

c is negative

Factorize (a) x² + 2x − 15 (b) x² − x − 20.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) The numbers multiply to −15, so they have opposite signs. They add to +2, so the larger one is positive: 5 and −3.
  2. x² + 2x − 15 = (x + 5)(x − 3).
  3. (b) The numbers multiply to −20 and add to −1, so the larger one is negative: −5 and 4.
  4. x² − x − 20 = (x − 5)(x + 4).

Answer: (a) (x + 5)(x − 3) (b) (x − 5)(x + 4)

Example 5.5

Splitting the middle term

Factorize x² + 5x + 6 by splitting the middle term and grouping.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Use the numbers 2 and 3 to split 5x: x² + 2x + 3x + 6.
  2. Group: x(x + 2) + 3(x + 2).
  3. Take out the common bracket: (x + 2)(x + 3). This is the same answer as Example 5.1.

Answer: (x + 2)(x + 3)

Common mistakes

  • Finding numbers that add to c and multiply to b (the wrong way round).
  • Getting the signs wrong when c is negative. Always expand to check.
  • Forgetting to take out a common factor first, e.g. in 2x² + 10x + 12.

Lesson 6 of 6

Factorizing ax² + bx + c and Choosing a Method

Builds on: Lessons 1–5, especially grouping (Lesson 2) and splitting the middle term (Lesson 5).

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can factorize quadratics where the coefficient of x² is not 1, and choose the right method for any expression.

1 Key ideas

  • Method 1: inspection (trial). Try pairs of brackets whose first terms multiply to ax² and whose last terms multiply to c. Expand to check the middle term. For example, 3x² + 22x + 24 = (3x + 4)(x + 6).
  • Method 2: the AC method (splitting the middle term). This is reliable and needs no guessing:
  • (1) Multiply a × c. (2) Find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b. (3) Use them to split the middle term. (4) Group and factorize, as in Lesson 2.
  • For 3x² + 22x + 24: ac = 72, and 4 × 18 = 72 with 4 + 18 = 22. So 3x² + 4x + 18x + 24 = x(3x + 4) + 6(3x + 4) = (3x + 4)(x + 6).
  • A box (grid) method is a neat way to organise the four terms. Put ax² and c in opposite corners and the two split terms in the others.
  • Choosing a method. (1) Always take out a common factor first. (2) Two terms: difference of two squares? (3) Three terms: perfect square? If not, use inspection or the AC method. (4) Four terms: grouping. (5) Check by expanding, and check that no bracket can be factorized further.
  • Some quadratics, such as 3x² + 4x + 5, cannot be factorized with whole numbers. Completing the square (Unit 8) can handle these.

Example 6.1

The AC method

Factorize 2x² + 7x + 3.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. a × c = 2 × 3 = 6. We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 7: 1 and 6.
  2. Split the middle term: 2x² + x + 6x + 3.
  3. Group: x(2x + 1) + 3(2x + 1).
  4. Take out the common bracket: (2x + 1)(x + 3).
  5. Check: 2x² + 6x + x + 3 = 2x² + 7x + 3 ✓

Answer: (2x + 1)(x + 3)

Example 6.2

The box method

Factorize 3x² + 22x + 24.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. ac = 3 × 24 = 72. Pairs: 4 × 18 = 72 and 4 + 18 = 22 ✓.
  2. Put 3x² and 24 in opposite corners of a 2 × 2 box, and 4x and 18x in the other two corners.
  3. Take the HCF of each row and column: the rows give x and 6, and the columns give 3x and 4.
  4. So 3x² + 22x + 24 = (3x + 4)(x + 6).

Answer: (3x + 4)(x + 6)

Example 6.3

A negative constant

Factorize 6x² − x − 2.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. ac = 6 × (−2) = −12. We need two numbers that multiply to −12 and add to −1: −4 and 3.
  2. Split: 6x² − 4x + 3x − 2.
  3. Group: 2x(3x − 2) + 1(3x − 2).
  4. Answer: (3x − 2)(2x + 1).

Answer: (3x − 2)(2x + 1)

Example 6.4

Inspection

Factorize 5x² + 11x + 2 by inspection.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. The first terms must be 5x and x. The last terms must be 1 and 2 (in some order).
  2. Try (5x + 1)(x + 2): the middle term is 10x + x = 11x ✓.
  3. (Trying (5x + 2)(x + 1) gives 5x + 2x = 7x ✗.)

Answer: (5x + 1)(x + 2)

Example 6.5

Choosing a method

Factorize completely: (a) 3x² − 12 (b) 2x² − 8x + 8 (c) 4x² + 8x − 12 (d) ax + bx − 2a − 2b

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Common factor 3: 3(x² − 4). Difference of squares: 3(x − 2)(x + 2).
  2. (b) Common factor 2: 2(x² − 4x + 4). Perfect square: 2(x − 2)².
  3. (c) Common factor 4: 4(x² + 2x − 3). Inspection (3 and −1): 4(x + 3)(x − 1).
  4. (d) Four terms, so group: x(a + b) − 2(a + b) = (a + b)(x − 2).

Answer: (a) 3(x − 2)(x + 2) (b) 2(x − 2)² (c) 4(x + 3)(x − 1) (d) (a + b)(x − 2)

Think: In every part, why did we look for a common factor before anything else?

Common mistakes

  • Finding numbers that multiply to c instead of a × c.
  • Stopping before the answer is complete, e.g. (2x + 4)(x + 3) still has a common factor of 2.
  • Not checking by expanding. One expansion catches most sign errors.
