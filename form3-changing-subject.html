Lesson 1 of 4

Formulae and the Subject

Builds on: Unit 2, Lesson 5: solving equations with inverse operations.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can explain what the subject of a formula is, and change the subject in one step.

1 Key ideas

  • A formula connects quantities. For example, A = lw gives the area of a rectangle.
  • The subject is the letter on its own on one side of the formula. In A = lw, A is the subject.
  • Changing the subject means rearranging the formula so that a different letter is on its own. For example, A = lw becomes w = A/l.
  • It works exactly like solving an equation. The only difference is that the answer contains letters instead of a number. Do the same thing to both sides, using inverse operations.
  • + and − undo each other, and × and ÷ undo each other.
  • Once you have rearranged a formula, you can substitute numbers to find the new subject.

3 Worked examples

Example 1.1

An equation and a formula side by side

(a) Solve 12 = x + 5. (b) Make x the subject of y = x + 5.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) To undo + 5, subtract 5 from both sides: 12 − 5 = x, so x = 7.
  2. (b) Do exactly the same: y − 5 = x.
  3. Write the subject on the left: x = y − 5.

Answer: (a) x = 7 (b) x = y − 5

Think: Substitute y = 12 into your answer to (b). Do you get the answer to (a)? Why?

Example 1.2

Undoing multiplication

Make I the subject of V = IR.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. IR means I × R. To leave I on its own, undo × R.
  2. Divide both sides by R: V/R = I.

Answer: I = V/R

Example 1.3

Rearrange, then substitute

The circumference of a circle is C = πd. (a) Make d the subject. (b) Find d when C = 44 cm. Use π = 22/7.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Divide both sides by π: d = C/π.
  2. (b) d = 44 ÷ 22/7 = 44 × 7/22 = 14.

Answer: (a) d = C/π (b) d = 14 cm

Example 1.4

Undoing division

Make n the subject of m = n/4.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. n is divided by 4. To undo ÷ 4, multiply both sides by 4.
  2. 4m = n.

Answer: n = 4m

Example 1.5

When the new subject is subtracted

a = b − c. Make (a) b the subject (b) c the subject.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Add c to both sides: a + c = b. So b = a + c.
  2. (b) c is being taken away, so first add c to both sides to make it positive: a + c = b.
  3. Then subtract a from both sides: c = b − a.

Answer: (a) b = a + c (b) c = b − a

Think: Check with numbers: 7 = 10 − 3. Does c = b − a give 3?

Common mistakes

  • Doing an operation to only one side of the formula.
  • Using the operation instead of its inverse, e.g. writing I = VR from V = IR.
  • Leaving a minus sign in front of the subject, e.g. −c = a − b. Make the subject positive.

Lesson 2 of 4

Two-Step Rearranging with Function Machines

Builds on: Lesson 1 and Unit 2, Lesson 5: the function machine for two-step equations.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can use a function machine or inverse operations to change the subject in two steps.

1 Key ideas

  • Build a function machine for the new subject. Write down what happens to it, in order, to make the other side of the formula.
  • To reverse the machine, start from the other end. Use the inverse of each operation, in reverse order.
  • For y = 3x + 5: x is × 3, then + 5. Going back: − 5, then ÷ 3. So x = (y − 5)/3.
  • Use brackets when you divide a whole expression: (y − 5)/3 is not the same as y − 5/3.
  • You can also use the balance method: do the same operation to both sides, one step at a time. Both methods give the same answer.
  • If the subject has a negative coefficient, as in y = 5 − 2x, add the x term to both sides first to make it positive.

3 Worked examples

Example 2.1

Using a function machine

Make x the subject of y = 3x + 5.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Forwards: x is multiplied by 3, then 5 is added, to give y.
  2. Backwards: start with y. Undo + 5 first: y − 5.
  3. Then undo × 3: (y − 5)/3.
  4. Check with x = 5: y = 20, and (20 − 5)/3 = 5 ✓

Answer: x = (y − 5)/3

Example 2.2

A physics formula

Make t the subject of v = u + at.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. What happens to t? It is multiplied by a, then u is added.
  2. Backwards: subtract u from both sides: v − u = at.
  3. Divide both sides by a: t = (v − u)/a.

Answer: t = (v − u)/a

Example 2.3

Perimeter of a rectangle

Make l the subject of P = 2l + 2w.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Subtract 2w from both sides: P − 2w = 2l.
  2. Divide both sides by 2: l = (P − 2w)/2.

Answer: l = (P − 2w)/2

Think: This can also be written l = P/2 − w. Why are these the same?

Example 2.4

A negative coefficient

Make x the subject of y = 5 − 2x.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Add 2x to both sides to make the x term positive: y + 2x = 5.
  2. Subtract y from both sides: 2x = 5 − y.
  3. Divide by 2: x = (5 − y)/2.
  4. Check with x = 1: y = 3, and (5 − 3)/2 = 1 ✓

Answer: x = (5 − y)/2

Example 2.5

Rearrange and use

The area of a triangle is A = ½bh. (a) Make h the subject. (b) Find h when A = 24 cm² and b = 8 cm.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) ½bh means b × h ÷ 2. Undo ÷ 2 first: 2A = bh.
  2. Then undo × b: h = 2A/b.
  3. (b) h = 2 × 24 ÷ 8 = 6.

Answer: (a) h = 2A/b (b) h = 6 cm

Common mistakes

  • Undoing the operations in the wrong order, e.g. x = y/3 − 5 for y = 3x + 5.
  • Forgetting brackets: writing y − 5/3 when you mean (y − 5)/3.
  • Sign errors when the subject has a negative coefficient.

Lesson 3 of 4

Formulae with Brackets and Fractions

Builds on: Lesson 2: two-step rearranging. Unit 2, Lesson 2: expanding brackets.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can change the subject of a formula that contains brackets or fractions.

1 Key ideas

  • Brackets: you can either divide by the number outside the bracket first, or expand the bracket first. Both methods work.
  • Fractions: multiply both sides by the denominator first to clear the fraction. For y = (x + 3)/4, multiply by 4 to get 4y = x + 3, so x = 4y − 3.
  • Work step by step, and write each new line underneath the one before.
  • Subject in the denominator: in y = 12/x, multiply both sides by x to get xy = 12, then divide by y to get x = 12/y.
  • Check your new formula by substituting simple numbers into both versions.

3 Worked examples

Example 3.1

A formula with a bracket

Make l the subject of P = 2(l + w).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Method 1: divide both sides by 2: P/2 = l + w. Subtract w: l = P/2 − w.
  2. Method 2: expand: P = 2l + 2w. Subtract 2w: P − 2w = 2l. Divide by 2: l = (P − 2w)/2.
  3. Both answers are the same formula.

Answer: l = P/2 − w (or l = (P − 2w)/2)

Example 3.2

Clearing a fraction

Make x the subject of y = (x + 3)/4.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Multiply both sides by 4: 4y = x + 3.
  2. Subtract 3: x = 4y − 3.
  3. Check with x = 5: y = 8/4 = 2, and 4 × 2 − 3 = 5 ✓

Answer: x = 4y − 3

Example 3.3

Fahrenheit and Celsius

F = 9C/5 + 32 converts °C to °F. (a) Make C the subject. (b) A hot day in St John's is 86°F. Convert this to °C.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Subtract 32: F − 32 = 9C/5.
  2. Multiply by 5: 5(F − 32) = 9C.
  3. Divide by 9: C = 5(F − 32)/9.
  4. (b) C = 5 × (86 − 32)/9 = 5 × 54/9 = 5 × 6 = 30.

Answer: (a) C = 5(F − 32)/9 (b) 30°C

Example 3.4

Area of a trapezium

Make a the subject of A = ½(a + b)h.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Multiply by 2: 2A = (a + b)h.
  2. Divide by h: 2A/h = a + b.
  3. Subtract b: a = 2A/h − b.

Answer: a = 2A/h − b

Example 3.5

Subject in the denominator

A 60 km journey at a speed of n km/h takes T = 60/n hours. Make n the subject.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Multiply both sides by n: Tn = 60.
  2. Divide both sides by T: n = 60/T.

Answer: n = 60/T

Think: Notice that T and n swap places. Does this always happen with formulae like y = k/x?

Common mistakes

  • Only multiplying one term by the denominator.
  • Expanding a bracket incorrectly, e.g. 2(l + w) = 2l + w.
  • Forgetting that 9C/5 means 9 × C ÷ 5, so both the × 9 and the ÷ 5 must be undone.

Lesson 4 of 4

Formulae with Powers and Roots

Builds on: Lessons 2 and 3, and square numbers and square roots from the warm-up.

Goal: By the end of this lesson I can change the subject of a formula that contains squares, cubes or roots.

1 Key ideas

  • Squaring and square-rooting undo each other. Cubing and cube-rooting undo each other.
  • If the new subject is squared, get the squared term on its own first, then take the square root of both sides. For A = πr², r² = A/π, so r = √(A/π).
  • If the new subject is under a square root, get the root on its own first, then square both sides. For y = √(x + 5), y² = x + 5, so x = y² − 5.
  • A function machine still works. Square and √ are just operations like × and +.
  • Strictly, x² = 9 gives x = 3 or x = −3 (written x = ±3). For lengths, times and other quantities that must be positive, use the positive root.

3 Worked examples

Example 4.1

Area of a circle

A = πr². (a) Make r the subject. (b) Find r when A = 154 cm². Use π = 22/7.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) r is squared, then multiplied by π. Undo × π: r² = A/π.
  2. Undo the square with a square root: r = √(A/π).
  3. (b) r² = 154 ÷ 22/7 = 154 × 7/22 = 49, so r = √49 = 7.

Answer: (a) r = √(A/π) (b) r = 7 cm

Example 4.2

The subject under a root

Make x the subject of (a) y = √x (b) y = √(x + 5).

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Square both sides: y² = x.
  2. (b) Square both sides: y² = x + 5. Subtract 5: x = y² − 5.

Answer: (a) x = y² (b) x = y² − 5

Example 4.3

Pythagoras' theorem

c² = a² + b². (a) Make a the subject. (b) Find a when c = 13 and b = 5.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Subtract b²: a² = c² − b². Square root: a = √(c² − b²).
  2. (b) a = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12.

Answer: (a) a = √(c² − b²) (b) a = 12

Think: Why is √(c² − b²) not the same as c − b? Test with c = 13 and b = 5.

Example 4.4

Two subjects from one formula

v² = u² + 2as. Make (a) s the subject (b) u the subject.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. (a) Subtract u²: v² − u² = 2as. Divide by 2a: s = (v² − u²)/(2a).
  2. (b) Subtract 2as: u² = v² − 2as. Square root: u = √(v² − 2as).

Answer: (a) s = (v² − u²)/(2a) (b) u = √(v² − 2as)

Example 4.5

The pendulum formula

The time for one swing of a pendulum is T = 2π√(L/g). Make L the subject.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Forwards: L is ÷ g, then square-rooted, then × 2π.
  2. Backwards: divide by 2π: T/(2π) = √(L/g).
  3. Square both sides: T²/(4π²) = L/g.
  4. Multiply by g: L = gT²/(4π²).

Answer: L = gT²/(4π²)

Example 4.6

Cube roots

The volume of a cube is V = x³. Make x the subject, and find x when V = 64 cm³.

Try it in your book first. Then reveal the solution one step at a time and compare it with your working.

  1. Take the cube root of both sides: x = ∛V.
  2. x = ∛64 = 4, because 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

Answer: x = ∛V; x = 4 cm

Common mistakes

  • Taking the square root too early, e.g. y = x² + 4 → x = √y + 4 ✗. First subtract 4: x = √(y − 4).
  • Writing √(c² − b²) = c − b ✗.
  • Square-rooting only part of an expression: √(A/π) is not √A/π.
